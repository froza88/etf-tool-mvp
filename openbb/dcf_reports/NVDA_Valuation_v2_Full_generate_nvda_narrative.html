3. The Back Story

Company Overview

NVIDIA Corporation was founded by Jensen Huang in 1993 to build graphics processors for gaming. For most of its first two decades, that is exactly what it did — surviving brutal competition from ATI (later AMD) and Intel. The pivot that changed everything was CUDA, introduced in 2006: a parallel-computing platform that allowed developers to write general-purpose code for NVIDIA GPUs. At the time it looked like a clever engineering move. In retrospect, it was one of the most consequential software decisions in the history of the semiconductor industry.

Today, NVIDIA is not a graphics chip company. Data Center revenue now represents approximately 92% of total company revenue, with an annualized run-rate above $300 billion. The company generated $215.9 billion in revenue in the most recent fiscal year, with operating margins of 60.4% and a return profile that places it among the most profitable large companies in history. With a market capitalization approaching $5 trillion, NVIDIA has become the toll road for AI infrastructure — a position that converts directly into cash: $50 billion in quarterly operating cash flow, 74% gross margins, and a balance sheet with more cash than debt. The question is not whether this position is valuable today; it is whether it is durable.

5. The Story

Possible: The full range of outcomes for NVIDIA spans from "dominant AI platform with sustained 60%+ margins" to "cyclical hardware vendor facing 30% margin compression as custom ASICs take share." At one end, AI compute becomes as essential as electricity, and NVIDIA collects a permanent toll on every token processed — justifying a terminal value that dominates the valuation. At the other end, the current buildout proves to be a one-time infrastructure cycle, and NVIDIA's pricing power erodes as customers deploy their own silicon. Between these extremes lies the answer.

Plausible: NVIDIA becomes the dominant but less monopolistic backbone of AI compute. Hyperscaler spending evolves from a panic buildout into a recurring replacement-and-expansion cycle as AI clusters age, model architectures advance, and inference scales to sovereign, enterprise, and physical AI workloads. The terminal growth rate of 3.5% caps the perpetuity at a plausible level for a company that would be among the largest in history. This story — dominance with normalization — produces an intrinsic value of $105.09 per share. It is more conservative than the Street consensus, and I am comfortable with that conservatism.

The physical sanity check matters: can any company maintain 3.5% perpetual growth from a base this large? 4. Cost of Capital: 10.5% (WACC). This valuation uses the Damodaran Semiconductor industry median WACC. NVIDIA's actual risk profile may differ — the stock's raw beta of 2.21 suggests much higher volatility, but bottom-up industry beta of 1.52 provides a more stable anchor. The 10.5% reflects a balanced view of the risks.
